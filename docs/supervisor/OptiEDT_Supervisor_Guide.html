The problem

Building a timetable by hand does not scale

Many teachers + Many groups + Many rooms + Many time slots + Many rules │ ▼ Conflicts, and weeks of manual work

A department normally builds its weekly timetable in a spreadsheet. There is no single person who can hold every rule in their head at once — a teacher cannot be in two rooms simultaneously, a room cannot host two sessions at once, a group cannot attend two lectures at the same hour, and every room needs the right type and enough seats.

The pipeline

What OptiEDT does

Data → Check five arithmetic checks: is this even possible? → Optimize CP-SAT places all 218 sessions under 12 unbreakable rules → 3 Candidates three different priority weightings, three valid timetables → Score & Rank each scored 0–100 on seven quality measures → Compare the officer sees exactly why one outranks another → Publish the department adopts one → Student view the group's own week, nothing else

Stages 1 and 2 always run. A third stage — diagnosis — runs only if no valid timetable exists at all: instead of a bare failure, it reports the smallest set of conflicting rules, so the officer knows exactly what to change.

Solving takes a few minutes on capable hardware, so the browser never sits waiting for it — a request returns immediately with a reference number, and the screen checks on progress.

Generation

Generating a timetable

SCREENSHOT — Generate screen, a completed run Capture: sign in as Timetable Officer, open Generate. Shows the pipeline stages and the three ranked candidates with their scores.
  1. The officer chooses a search effort and presses Generate timetable.
  2. Stage 1 checks the data — is a solution even possible?
  3. Stage 2 runs CP-SAT three times, under three different priority weightings.
  4. Each result is scored on seven quality measures and ranked.
  5. The three candidates appear, best first.

This takes time because CP-SAT is genuinely searching a very large space for a provably valid answer, not approximating one. On the developer's own machine, using every processor core, the reference run above completed in 287 seconds. On this deployment's free hosting tier — a small fraction of a CPU core — the same search can take twenty minutes or more. That is a hosting-plan limit, not a limit of the algorithm.

The rules that cannot be broken

Hard constraints

A hard constraint is a rule with no exceptions. If a timetable would break one, it is not produced — not scored down, not accepted as "close enough". Twelve such rules govern every timetable OptiEDT builds:

CodeRule
H1A teacher cannot teach two sessions at once
H2A group cannot attend two sessions at once
H3A room cannot host two sessions at once
H4A session must be placed in a room of the right type
H5A room must be large enough for the group
H6A teacher's declared unavailability must be respected
H7Every session gets exactly one time and one room
H8A multi-hour session stays within a single day
H9Nothing is placed on a closed slot or holiday
H10A session the officer has locked stays where it is
H11Total demand never exceeds total room capacity
H12If a whole year group is busy, its sub-groups are too
If these twelve rules cannot all be satisfied together, OptiEDT reports exactly which ones conflict, rather than returning an invalid timetable or searching forever.
Beyond "valid"

Quality and ranking

A valid timetable is not automatically the best one. Among all the timetables that break none of the twelve rules, some are still better than others — fewer gaps in a student's day, more teachers getting their preferred hours, and so on. OptiEDT generates three valid candidates under three different priorities and scores each one out of 100:

CandidatePriorityScore
Candidate 1Balanced81.438
Candidate 2Student-favouring80.572
Candidate 3Teacher-favouring80.611

The score is a plain weighted sum of seven measures — nothing more elaborate, deliberately. Because it is only a sum, it can be checked with a calculator:

That is the exact figure the live system displays for candidate 1 — not an approximation, the same number to the last decimal.

Understanding the ranking

The Compare screen

SCREENSHOT — Compare screen, decomposition ledger Capture: sign in as Timetable Officer, open Compare, select two candidates. Shows the two scores side by side and the criterion-by-criterion breakdown.

This screen answers the question a department head will always ask: why is this one ranked above the other? Rather than a summary, it shows the actual calculation — each of the seven quality measures contributes a signed amount to the score difference, and every contribution is added up in front of the reader:

CriterionContribution
Student idle time (S2)+1.736
Teacher idle time (S3)−0.310
Teacher preference (S5)−0.612
Sum of contributions+0.827
Difference in score+0.827

These two numbers are always identical — verified against the live system at zero error. The explanation is not a summary of the calculation; it is the calculation, read term by term.

Explanation, not decision

The AI assistant

SCREENSHOT — the "OptiEDT AI" panel on the Compare screen Capture: on Compare, with two candidates selected, alongside the decomposition table on the right-hand side. Shows the label "Written by the language service" above an answer.
What it doesWhat it never does
Explains why a candidate scores as it doesGenerate the timetable
Answers questions about the run's own figuresChange a score or the ranking
Summarises a candidate's strengths and weaknessesMove or edit a session
Suggests a change for a human to approveTouch the database

Optimization and AI are kept strictly separate. The optimizer decides what is a valid timetable. The scoring engine decides what is a better one. The AI only reads numbers already produced by those two and writes sentences about them — every figure it states is checked against the numbers it was given, and an answer with an invented figure is discarded entirely rather than shown with one wrong number in it.

Tested directly against the live system: asked a question its supplied data could not answer, the assistant replied that the information was not available — rather than guessing. That refusal is the safeguard working as intended.
How it is built

Architecture

User's Browser │ ▼ Vercel (the React interface, served as static files) │ ▼ Render (the FastAPI backend) │ ▼ Supabase (PostgreSQL — runs, timetables, accounts, publications) From the backend: FastAPI → OR-Tools CP-SAT (places every session) FastAPI → Groq AI Assistant (explains, never decides)

Every request from the browser goes to Vercel, which forwards anything meant for the backend straight through to Render. The frontend never talks to the database or the solver directly — only the backend does, which is what keeps the guarantees in section 6 enforceable rather than optional.

Why these tools

Technology choices

TechnologyWhy it was chosen

Why not host everything on Vercel?

Vercel runs code in short-lived functions that are stopped after seconds. A CP-SAT solve takes minutes in a background process. Checked directly against Vercel's own documentation before deciding: the function would be killed mid-solve, a second request could land on a different instance and lose the result, and the "one solve at a time" rule the system depends on would break under load. The fix needed one small configuration file and no change to the application itself.

Real engineering

Problems and solutions

1. A reassuring number was the wrong measure

Discovered: overall week occupancy read as 71% — comfortable. Investigation: the real risk sits in two-hour windows, which were at 91% with almost no spare capacity. Result: the instance was nearly infeasible, and I had trusted the easy number instead of trying to disprove it. Cost several days.

2. The solver's own reported score didn't match its answer

Discovered: the score the solver library reported and the score its own solution actually earned disagreed. Investigation: the scoring engine recomputes every score independently from the real placements rather than trusting the solver's internal figure. Result: that independence caught a genuine bug in the third-party library — nothing in the product broke.

3. Docker looked successful while reaching the wrong database

Discovered: another PostgreSQL was already running on the same port. Investigation: the container reported "healthy" while every connection silently reached the other server. Result: the port is now configurable, and the exact server version is checked before trusting a connection.

4. One hard-coded setting blocked deployment entirely

Discovered: the list of websites allowed to call the API was a fixed value in the source code. Investigation: it worked on the developer's machine and nowhere else. Result: made configurable — the one change that unblocked deployment.

5. The obvious hosting choice was the wrong one

Discovered: Vercel seemed like the natural place for the whole application. Investigation: its functions are killed after seconds; the solver runs for minutes. Result: split the deployment — frontend on Vercel, backend on a server that stays alive — with one small configuration file and no change to the application's own code.

Speaking about it

Questions my supervisor may ask

What is OptiEDT?
A system that automatically builds a university department's weekly timetable, ranks several valid options, and explains the ranking in plain language.
What problem are you solving?
Manual timetabling takes weeks, depends on one person, and conflicts surface only after publication. OptiEDT builds a conflict-free timetable automatically in minutes.
Why is timetable generation difficult?
Hundreds of sessions, teachers, groups and rooms all have to satisfy many rules simultaneously — the number of possible arrangements is far beyond manual search.
Why did you use optimization instead of AI to build the timetable?
Because I needed a guarantee, not a good guess. A constraint solver makes an invalid timetable structurally impossible; a learned model would only make one less likely.
Why CP-SAT / OR-Tools specifically?
It is built exactly for this kind of problem — assigning things under hard rules — and is a mature, well-tested Google library.
What are hard constraints?
Rules that cannot be broken under any circumstance, like a teacher never teaching two sessions at once. Breaking one means the timetable isn't produced at all.
What are soft constraints?
Quality preferences, like minimising gaps in a student's day. They don't invalidate a timetable — they change its score.
Why generate three candidates instead of one?
Because "best" depends on priorities — favouring students, favouring teachers, or balancing both. All three are valid; the choice is a policy decision for a person, not the algorithm.
How is the score calculated?
A plain weighted sum of seven quality measures, each between 0 and 1, multiplied by its weight and summed to 100.
How do you know the score is correct?
Because it's just arithmetic — I recomputed it by hand from the published figures and it matched the system's number to the last digit.
Where is the AI?
Only in the explanations. It reads figures the system already calculated and writes sentences about them.
Can the AI change the timetable?
No. It cannot place a session, change a score, or touch the database — it only produces text, and even that text is checked against the real numbers before being shown.
Why keep optimization and AI separate?
So a mistake in the AI can only produce a wrong sentence, never a wrong timetable or a wrong score. The two failure modes are kept apart on purpose.
Why React/Vite for the frontend?
Fast to build with, and it compiles to plain static files that are cheap and simple to host.
Why FastAPI for the backend?
A Python framework, so it sits naturally beside the Python solver code.
Why PostgreSQL?
A reliable, well-understood relational database for runs, candidates and accounts.
Why Supabase?
Managed PostgreSQL hosting — no server to maintain myself.
Why Vercel?
Free, fast static hosting for the frontend, with automatic redeploys.
Why Render?
It runs a persistent process, which the solver needs — Vercel's functions get killed after seconds.
Why not host everything on Vercel?
A CP-SAT solve takes minutes; Vercel would kill it partway through every time.
What happens when I click Generate?
The system checks the data, then runs the solver three times under different priorities, scores each result, and ranks them.
What happens when a timetable is published?
It becomes visible to teachers and students, along with a full record of exactly which run and settings produced it.
What does a student see?
Only their own group's published timetable — nothing about other groups or unpublished drafts.
What was the hardest technical problem?
Trusting a reassuring occupancy number that was actually measuring the wrong thing, and nearly missing that the instance was close to infeasible.
What would you improve in a future version?
Persisting examination results, running on a server with a full CPU core, and giving every account its own individual password.

My 5-minute explanation

The problem. Manual timetabling for a department this size — 218 sessions, 44 teachers, 51 groups, 20 rooms — takes two to three weeks and conflicts still slip through.

My approach. I split the work into two separate decisions that never mix: what is a valid timetable, and what makes one valid timetable better than another.

Optimization. A constraint solver, OR-Tools CP-SAT, handles validity. It places every session under twelve rules that cannot be broken. If they can't all be satisfied, it reports exactly which rules conflict instead of guessing.

Ranking. Quality is a separate, much simpler question: a plain weighted sum of seven measures. Because it's just arithmetic, the difference between any two timetables can be broken down term by term and verified by hand — I did exactly that and it matched the system to the last digit.

The AI. It only explains results that already exist. It can't place a session, can't change a score, can't touch the database, and every number it writes is checked against the real figures before being shown.

Architecture. React on Vercel for the interface, FastAPI on Render for the backend and solver, PostgreSQL on Supabase for the data. Vercel couldn't run the solver itself — its functions get killed after seconds, and a solve takes minutes — so I checked that against Vercel's own documentation and split the deployment accordingly.

Problems I solved. I nearly missed that the instance was close to infeasible because I trusted a reassuring overall-occupancy number instead of the two-hour-window measure that actually mattered. I also caught a real bug in the solver library, because the scoring engine recomputes every score independently rather than trusting the solver's own number.

Where it stands. It's deployed and live right now, with real generated timetables you can inspect, compare and publish today.
